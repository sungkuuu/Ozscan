Did the grades predict anything?

A rating is only worth what it forecasts. So we graded wallets using data through June 30, sealed it, and scored those grades against July and August — a period the grading never saw.

The test

Grades were computed from settled outcomes in April 2 – June 30, 2026 and nothing else. They were then measured against realized results in July 1 – August 27, 2026. No wallet was re-graded using the test period, and no wallet was dropped for performing badly.

Where the wallets came from

Selection decides what a result like this can mean, so here is the whole of it. The universe is 1,329 addresses:

  • 960 seen in the live trade feed placing a single trade of $2,000 or more
  • 196 taken from Polymarket's own leaderboard
  • 90 taken from a builder's public smart-money leaderboard
  • 82 earlier wallets from the same trade feed, backfilled first
  • 1 featured as a top trader on a builder's front page

Wallets with fewer than 50 settled bets are not graded, which leaves 909 today. The walk forward used the 724 that cleared that floor on data through June 30.

This is not a random sample of Polymarket. Every wallet in it was already large, already ranked, or both — nobody enters by being interesting. Two of the sources rank by past profit, which is the selector this whole report argues against, so the A grade is separating inside a pool that has already been filtered that way rather than picking winners out of the general population.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. Nothing here speaks to wallets that no leaderboard has noticed and that never placed a $2,000 trade while the collector was watching — they were never eligible to be graded. And a wallet that stopped trading before collection began is absent, not failed, so the population is drawn from survivors of a window we did not choose.

What the grades forecast

"Hit rate" is the share of still-active wallets that finished the test period profitable. "Median wallet" is the middle wallet's return on stake — a check that the result isn't one lucky outlier. The last column is a different statistic, not that median with a row deleted: it removes the grade's single largest earner and then pools the profit and stake of every wallet left, which is what a follower spreading capital across the whole cohort would have made without the one wallet that carried it.

The finding

Headline result

23 of the 27 wallets we graded A were profitable out of sample.

The median A wallet returned +19.4% on stake. Pooled across the cohort with its single largest earner removed, the grade still returns +10% — a harder test than the median, and the relevant one if you would have spread capital over all of them rather than picked the winner in advance. Under a coin-flip assumption, 23 or more out of 27 lands around two in ten thousand.

Every other grade sits near a coin flip. That is the honest shape of the result: the signal lives at the top of the scale, not across it.

Could a follower have captured it?

Being right is not the same as being copyable. For each A-grade purchase we found the first trade another wallet actually executed in the same market and direction within the next five minutes, and used that as the price a follower could really have paid.

What the grade turned out to be selecting against

Nothing in the grade looks at what a market is about. It reads pace, concentration, entry level, sample size and settled return. This check, run 2026-08-28, asks what those inputs ended up excluding. It uses the same footing as the test above — the grades frozen at June 30, and only the bets they were computed from — and counts a bet as crypto up/down when its market slug matches /(btc|eth|sol|xrp|doge|bitcoin|ethereum|solana)[-_].*(up|down)|updown/i.

A and B wallets are effectively absent from that market — one bet in two thousand for A. D and F sit at roughly one in eleven and one in fifteen. The market resolves every fifteen minutes and is worked at machine pace, and the grade pushed the wallets living there down the scale without being told what the market was.

The pattern is not monotone. D carries a higher share than F, so this is not a category filter running quietly inside the grade. What separates cleanly is the top of the scale from the bottom half; the ordering within the bottom half is not explained by this at all.

It also bounds what outside data can fix. The one public archive of Polymarket order-book depth covers these crypto markets, and on this cohort 0.05% of what an A-grade wallet traded falls inside it. For the markets a followable wallet actually trades, depth history is not published anywhere.

What this does not show

It is conditional on liquidity. Only 61% of A-grade buys had another trade in the same market within five minutes. In the rest there may simply have been nothing to follow.

It is conditional on holding to resolution. A separate experiment measured what happens if you copy and then sell an hour later: the edge disappears. Short-horizon copying and outcome-horizon copying are different strategies, and only the second one survived.

It is conditional on the market staying uncrowded. The five-minute cushion exists because few people are currently racing to copy the same wallets. As copy trading grows — and as lists like ours get published — that cushion narrows.

B did not work, and F was not a loss. B-grade wallets hit 55% and turn negative once their best performer is removed. F-grade wallets finished slightly positive in aggregate. An F means the record gives no reason to expect it to repeat; it does not mean the wallet loses money.

One test period, thirty A-grade wallets, two months. Prices are recomputed from settled outcomes and public order history throughout. This is evidence, not a guarantee, and none of it is financial advice.

Checking it yourself

Everything needed to judge the design is on this page: the windows, the wallet universe and how it was filtered, the definition of every column, the rule that classifies a market, and the four conditions the result depends on. That is deliberate — a rating nobody can argue with is a rating nobody can use.

Recomputing the numbers line by line takes one more thing: the per-wallet record behind them. That file has a row for each of the 724 wallets in the cohort — the grade and score it carried on June 30, its settled bets in each window, and the stake, profit and return it produced in the test period. From it every figure in the table above can be rebuilt, including both readings of the last column.
